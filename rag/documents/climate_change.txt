CLIMATE CHANGE: CAUSES, EFFECTS AND RESPONSES
A short introduction (sample document for testing)

1. What is climate change?

Climate change refers to long-term shifts in the average temperature and weather patterns of the Earth. Natural factors such as volcanic eruptions, changes in solar activity and slow variations in the Earth's orbit have always influenced the climate. Since the Industrial Revolution, however, human activities have become the main driver of the warming observed over the past century. Scientists usually describe the change by comparing today's global average temperature with the "pre-industrial" average, meaning the period from roughly 1850 to 1900.

2. The greenhouse effect

The Earth receives energy from the Sun as visible light. The surface absorbs this light and gives off heat as infrared radiation. Certain gases in the atmosphere, called greenhouse gases, absorb part of this infrared radiation and send it back toward the surface. This natural greenhouse effect keeps the planet about 33 degrees Celsius warmer than it would otherwise be, which makes life possible.

The most important greenhouse gases are water vapour, carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O) and fluorinated gases used in refrigeration. When humans add more of these gases to the atmosphere, more heat is trapped and the planet warms. This is called the enhanced greenhouse effect.

3. Main human causes

Burning fossil fuels is the largest source of carbon dioxide. Coal, oil and natural gas are burned to generate electricity, power vehicles and run factories. Before the Industrial Revolution, the concentration of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere was about 280 parts per million (ppm). It has since risen above 420 ppm, the highest level in at least 800,000 years according to air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice cores.

Deforestation is the second major cause. Trees absorb carbon dioxide as they grow, so cutting down forests both removes this natural "carbon sink" and releases stored carbon when the wood is burned or decays.

Agriculture also contributes. Cattle and other ruminant animals produce methane during digestion, rice paddies release methane from flooded soils, and nitrogen fertilisers release nitrous oxide. Methane traps far more heat per molecule than carbon dioxide, although it stays in the atmosphere for a shorter time, roughly a decade.

4. Observed effects

The global average surface temperature has risen by more than 1 degree Celsius since pre-industrial times. Warming is not even across the planet: the Arctic is warming about three to four times faster than the global average, a pattern known as Arctic amplification.

Sea levels are rising for two reasons. First, water expands as it warms (thermal expansion). Second, glaciers and the ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica are melting and adding water to the oceans. Rising seas increase the risk of coastal flooding, especially for low-lying countries and cities on river deltas, such as Alexandria on the Nile Delta.

The oceans have absorbed about a quarter of the carbon dioxide released by humans. When carbon dioxide dissolves in seawater it forms carbonic acid, lowering the pH of the ocean. This process, called ocean acidification, makes it harder for corals, oysters and some plankton to build their shells and skeletons.

Extreme weather is becoming more frequent and intense in many regions. Heatwaves are hotter and longer, heavy rainfall events are stronger because warmer air holds more moisture (about 7 percent more for every degree Celsius of warming), and droughts are more severe in some dry regions.

5. International agreements

The United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) was adopted in 1992 and is the basis for international climate negotiations. Its member countries meet every year at a Conference of the Parties, known as a COP.

The Kyoto Protocol, adopted in 1997, set binding emission-reduction targets for developed countries.

The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015 at COP21, set the goal of keeping global warming well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, while pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5 degrees Celsius. Under the agreement, each country submits its own climate plan, called a Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC), and updates it every five years.

6. Mitigation and adaptation

Responses to climate change fall into two groups.

Mitigation means reducing the causes of climate change. Examples include replacing fossil fuels with renewable energy such as solar and wind power, improving energy efficiency in buildings and transport, switching to electric vehicles, protecting and replanting forests, and capturing carbon dioxide before it reaches the atmosphere.

Adaptation means adjusting to the effects that can no longer be avoided. Examples include building sea walls and flood defences, developing drought-resistant crops, improving early-warning systems for heatwaves and storms, and designing cities with more trees and shade to reduce urban heat.

Most experts agree that both are necessary: mitigation limits how much the climate will change, and adaptation reduces the damage from the changes that are already happening.

7. Summary

Climate change is driven mainly by greenhouse gases released by burning fossil fuels, deforestation and agriculture. Its effects include rising temperatures, sea-level rise, ocean acidification and more extreme weather. The Paris Agreement aims to limit warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius, and meeting that goal requires both cutting emissions and adapting to the changes already under way.
